# Week 5 Classroom Pilot and Validation Pack

**Course:** Computer Fundamentals and Artificial Intelligence Foundation Course  
**Academy:** Next Generation Academy  
**Week:** 5 — AI Foundations and Professional Prompt Engineering  
**Lessons:** 21–25  
**Planned teaching time:** 10 hours (five lessons × 120 minutes)  
**Status:** Ready for instructor pre-check; classroom evidence must be completed after teaching.

---

## 1. Purpose

Use this pack to test whether Week 5 is understandable, appropriately paced, safe and practical for students who have completed the academy's Basic Computer & AI Foundation Course.

A document review cannot prove that students have learned the material. The instructor must observe a real class, collect student feedback and record any changes needed.

## 2. What the pilot should confirm

By the end of the week, students should be able to:
1. Explain AI in simple language and identify everyday uses, benefits and limitations.
2. Distinguish AI, machine learning, deep learning and large language models at a beginner level.
3. Explain the difference between an AI model, an assistant application and optional integrations/tools.
4. Use a clear prompt with a task, context, audience, constraints and output format.
5. Use Prompt Coach to improve a rough idea without losing the original intention.
6. Identify unsupported assumptions added by an AI assistant and correct them.
7. Refine an output with a focused follow-up instruction.
8. Verify important claims and protect private information.

## 4. Five-lesson pilot observation plan

Use one observation sheet per lesson. Record actual timings rather than assuming that every activity fits exactly.

| Lesson | Main evidence to observe | Possible warning sign |
|---|---|---|
| 21 — Introduction to AI | Students explain AI examples, benefits, risks and human review | Students describe AI as always correct or human-like |
| 22 — AI, ML, DL and LLMs | Students sort concepts and explain training, inference, tokens and hallucinations simply | Students confuse an AI app with the underlying model or memorize terms without examples |
| 23 — AI assistants and ecosystem | Students compare assistants fairly and identify privacy/permission concerns | Students assume every tool has the same features or upload sensitive data |
| 24 — Prompt Engineering Part 1 | Students build prompts and use Prompt Coach to improve a rough idea | Prompt Coach changes the goal or invents audience, facts or requirements that students accept |
| 25 — Prompt Engineering Part 2 | Students revise outputs, test prompts and maintain a prompt library | Students accept the first answer, use vague “make it better” revisions or fail to verify claims |

### Standard lesson timing reference

The lesson package uses a 120-minute plan:
- 0–10 minutes: welcome, connect to prior learning and outcomes.
- 10–20 minutes: key concepts.
- 20–35 minutes: instructor demonstration.
- 35–80 minutes: guided practical activity.
- 80–105 minutes: independent challenge, peer review and verification.
- 105–115 minutes: misconceptions, privacy and responsible use.
- 115–120 minutes: exit question and homework.

If the class needs more time, record which task took longer and what students found difficult. Do not rush privacy, safety or verification to finish a worksheet.

## 5. Prompt Coach practical pilot

Use this same starter idea with the class:

**Rough idea:** “Make a presentation about computers.”

Ask students to:
1. Write what they think is missing before using Prompt Coach.
2. Submit the rough idea to the reusable Prompt Coach template.
3. Read the improved prompt and any alternatives.
4. Highlight details that were genuinely helpful.
5. Circle any invented or incorrect assumptions.
6. Choose or edit a version that preserves the original intention.
7. Test the selected prompt using an approved tool or teacher-provided output.
8. Check whether the result follows the topic, audience, slide count and other requirements.
9. Write one focused revision instruction.

**Expected evidence:** The final prompt states a suitable audience, purpose, scope, output format and constraints. The student can explain why the changes help and identify at least one limitation or check.

**Do not require identical wording.** Accept different prompts if they preserve the student's goal and are clear, relevant and testable.

## 8. Pilot review and decision criteria

After teaching, review the evidence. These are suggested internal course-improvement thresholds, not formal accreditation standards.

| Area | Suggested review signal | Action if not met |
|---|---|---|
| Lesson completion | All essential outcomes taught without skipping safety/verification | Adjust timing or split an activity across lessons |
| Student understanding | At least 80% of students demonstrate the essential skill independently or with only minor support | Re-teach the concept, simplify examples and add guided practice |
| Prompt Coach | At least 80% can identify an invented assumption or explain why a rewrite needs review | Add a worked example and a short assumption-check exercise |
| Practical access | Every student has a workable route to complete tasks, including offline fallback | Use instructor demonstration, printed tasks or approved alternatives |
| Privacy and safety | No student is instructed to share sensitive information | Stop, correct the activity and review the safety briefing |
| Workload | Most students complete the core task within the planned session or a clearly recorded adjustment | Reduce repetition or move extension tasks to homework |

A student who needs support should receive guided practice, not be labelled as incapable. Record the specific skill and provide a recheck opportunity.
